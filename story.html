The story

Music down the telephone line.

Long before streaming, a radio in a Spanish home got its music from the telephone wire. This is one of those radios, and how it came to play again.

Bern, around 1964

A receiver for Hilo Musical

The Hasler HM was built in Bern for Telefónica’s Hilo Musical, a service that sent music into homes over the telephone line. A row of piano keys chose one of six music channels, a volume knob and a tone knob did the rest, and a small pilot lamp in its window said the set was on.

The service went quiet, as wired music did everywhere. The cabinets stayed: solid, handsome, built to last, and with nothing left to play.

Why restore it

Because the radio was never the problem.

The keys still worked. The knobs still turned. The speaker still had its voice. Only the music had gone away.

So we didn’t gut it and fit a tablet in the front. We gave it a new source of music and left everything you touch, see and hear where it was. Its key page on your phone is a help, never a requirement: you can live with a Hasler for years without opening it.

The old parts are protected, not used up. The volume has a ceiling set for this speaker, so a 1964 cone never works harder than it should. The tone knob’s effects never make it work harder either.

The choices we made

Four rules, kept every time.

From the outside, it is the old radio

Press a key and music plays. Press the playing key again and it sleeps. Turn the tone knob left until it clicks and it switches off. The pilot lamp tells you what it is doing, the way a valve radio’s lamp did, and when it goes dark it is safe to unplug.

No accounts

There is no Hasler account and nothing to register. The radio belongs to the person it lives with. For the few things that change a lot, its page asks for a press of a key on the radio itself: proof that someone is standing next to it.

Nothing in the cloud needed to play

Stations, keys, settings and your shortlist all live on the radio. If our servers vanished tomorrow, it would keep playing your stations, your playlists, your phones and your rooms, on the version it has.

Honest, plain language

No jargon on the radio’s page, and no promises it can’t keep. When something is wrong, it says what, in words, with one thing to try. Where it trusts your home network, as a printer does, it says so in one plain line.

The makers

Delgado & Murphy

Two people, one bench. We restore the cabinets, write the software that lives inside them, design the circuit board that joins the old parts to the new, and answer when an owner writes.

Everything is open: the code, the board, the guides. If you have an old radio of your own, the makers’ page is where to start.

More cabinets are coming

An Excelsior is next on the bench, with ten keys that stay down and four wheels. Others are waiting. Each cabinet’s panel is a short description the same program reads, so a new radio is a new description, not a new piece of software.
